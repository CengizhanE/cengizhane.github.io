Gezgin - Nanosatellite Launch from the Stratosphere

Rockoon Architecture, ITU Seed, My First Startup

Shortest Background Story

Ever since high-school, I was obsessed with space and rockets. I watched the first launch of Falcon Heavy and it changed something in me. I had never thought space was this close.

Figure 1: Falcon Heavy Launch Credit:Images courtesy SpaceX

Figure 2: Falcon Heavy Booster Catch Credit:Images courtesy SpaceX

These were my wallpaper for a long time.

This is where I thought I might be the one who designs and launches these rockets.

I was studying for the university entrance exam, and my only goal was getting into İTÜ Astronautical Engineering.

After taking the exam and getting my results, I had one and only choice in my head. I wrote İTÜ Astronautical Engineering (AE) on my preference list and nothing else.

Then, the long wait started…

After a few weeks, I learned that I got into İTÜ AE. I was happier than ever. I had finally achieved what I’ve been telling myself I would do every single night for months.

Next mission was English. I got into the English preparation classes, but we had to take a break because of the coronavirus. I ended up continuing the first year of my engineering degree completely from home.

The Idea I Almost Didn’t Take Seriously

When I was stuck at home, right after an online class, a friend and classmate pitched me the concept. At first, I didn’t understand it at all. I asked “What the hell can I do to help?” and he simply said that I would be helpful. I guess he was right. This was probably one of the turning points in my life.

When I started listening to him and reading more I kinda loved the concept.

But…

It was too good to be true. So we started running the numbers. (Engineering brain starts developing…)

I can simply explain the concept like this: A balloon-assisted rocket launch system "a rockoon" where a high-altitude gas balloon carries a small rocket to the stratosphere, and the rocket ignites from there.

This isn’t something we found. This system was very old. But they didn’t have 500+ nanosatellite launch demands per year. So we had the right timing. (or that was the thought we had back then)

It was crazy to think three 20-year-olds can create something this big but this was exactly what every entrepreneur did. As I said before we started doing math and physics to understand the project. The more I sat with the numbers, the more it made sense. If we can “simply” skip the dense atmosphere layer we can save that 50% of the fuel back. The important catch is, this isn’t just about fuel efficiency. When you don’t need to lift 50% of the fuel the system gets easier.

Yeah, at the end of the day, it is still rocket science.

But it’s easier.

Just a little bit.

The Reality of High-Altitude Engineering

When I say “easier” I only mean the fuel math.

In reality the system design brings a whole new set of challenges.

The stratosphere is around -50°C. The avionics will just freeze before the rocket even gets the chance to ignite.

Then… the separation part.

The plan was to ignite the rocket while it was still hanging from the balloon. But at 20-30 km there is roughly 1% of sea level pressure.

The fins can’t stabilize the rocket at that pressure. Therefore, system needs an active stabilization which also has to survive the cold.

And separation has to happen right after ignition since the balloon was part of the reusable architecture.

We realized pretty fast that doing the physics on a computer is one thing. But building a physical separation system that actually works is something else.

The “Other” Part

We were engineering students so we knew the math and physics parts. Or, we knew, we can learn it. But entrepreneurship was a whole another beast.

This was the “other” part of the project and we had no idea what we’re going into.

We thought best way to learn something is learning it from the best. So we tried applying to ITU Seed.

This part was the point where we built the whole structure of the Gezgin project. I cannot emphasize how much I learned about entrepreneurship in this journey. It was 5-6 months of straight learning, pivoting, talking to potential customers, pivoting, creating simulations, pivoting, reading, researching, building and pivoting…

It was an incredible journey.

Figure 3: Gezgin Logo That I Drew by Hand

And I remember the final day clear as yesterday. The moment where they announced the winners. We all held out breathes. Aaand…

Yes.

We did it.

We couldn’t get the financial aid but we got into the ecosystem. This was a real milestone. The program is competitive, so being accepted gave us credibility.

The business case we built was grounded in real market research. The small satellite launch market was growing really fast. The market leaders (SpaceX, Rocket Lab etc.) had pricing and scheduling gaps that a low-cost rockoon system could theoretically address.

After that, we entered competitions. We were trying to get money for prototyping, because you cannot validate a balloon rocket separation system with a bunch of calculations.

While doing these we expanded our team too. I remember we were 10+ students trying to make this a reality. But we couldn’t find the funding to build a prototype.

Why I Left? Did I Regret?

This part is hardest part to write.

I eventually left because I needed a break from university.

Trying to balance the brutal courses with the demands of a startup pushed my mental health to a place I didn’t like. I realized I needed a break.

So, did I regret it?

The answer is yes. And no.

I can fill 2 pages just to write what I feel.
